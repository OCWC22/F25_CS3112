**SOLUTIONS**

**Solution to 5.2-1: Probability of hiring exactly one time or exactly n times**

Step 0: Understand the setup (context and basics).  
   - We're analyzing the HIRE-ASSISTANT algorithm, where n candidates arrive in a random order (every permutation is equally likely, probability 1/n!).  
   - The algorithm: We always hire the first candidate (no "best" yet). Then, for each later candidate, we hire them only if they're better than all previous ones (the current best-so-far).  
   - Why random order? It makes the input distribution uniform, so we can use probability. How? We assume all orders are possible and equally likely.  
   - What we're finding: Exact probabilities for hiring k times, where k=1 or k=n.  
   - Fundamentals reminder: Probability = favorable outcomes / total outcomes. For hiring, it depends on where the best candidate is.

Step 1: Probability of exactly one hire (k=1).  
   - This happens only if the very best candidate is first, and no one else is better (but since they're the best, no one after is better).  
   - Why? The first hire is automatic. We only hire again if someone surpasses the current best. If the best is first, no one surpasses them.  
   - How to calculate: In a random order, the best candidate can be in any of the n positions, each with probability 1/n (by symmetry—every position is equally likely).  
   - So, P(best is first) = 1/n.  
   - Therefore, P(exactly one hire) = 1/n.  
   - Context: This is the best-case scenario for the algorithm—minimal hires.

Step 2: Probability of exactly n hires (k=n).  
   - This happens if every candidate is better than all previous ones—i.e., they arrive in strictly increasing quality order (1st is best of first 1, 2nd is best of first 2, etc., up to nth being best of all).  
   - Why? Each hire happens when the new candidate beats the current best-so-far. For n hires, every single one must beat the previous bests.  
   - How to calculate: There's exactly 1 such "strictly increasing" order out of n! possible orders (the sorted order).  
   - So, P(exactly n hires) = 1/n!.  
   - Context: This is the worst-case scenario—maximum hires.

**Solution to 5.2-2: Probability of hiring exactly twice**

Step 0: Understand when exactly two hires happen.  
   - Always hire the first candidate (hire #1).  
   - Need exactly one more hire later, and no further hires.  
   - Why only one more? If more than one additional hire, we'd have >2 hires. The additional hire must be the overall best, otherwise a later even-better candidate would cause a third hire.  
   - Context: This is like finding where the best candidate is, but with conditions on earlier candidates.

Step 1: Fix the position k of the overall best candidate (k from 2 to n).  
   - P(best in position k) = 1/n (uniform random).  
   - Among the first k-1 candidates, the first one must be the best of those k-1; otherwise, we'd hire someone else before k, leading to >2 hires.  
   - Why? If the first isn't the best of the first k-1, then when we reach the best of those k-1 (who isn't first), we'd hire them as a second hire before k, and then k (the overall best) would be a third hire.  
   - How: Given any set of k-1 candidates, each is equally likely to be the best of them, so P(first is best of first k-1) = 1/(k-1).  
   - Context: We're conditioning on the best being at k, and ensuring no early hires disrupt the count.

Step 2: Combine probabilities over all k.  
   - For each k, joint P = (1/n) * (1/(k-1)).  
   - Sum over k=2 to n: (1/n) * (1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/(n-1)) = (H_{n-1})/n, where H_{n-1} is the (n-1)th harmonic number.  
   - Why sum? Because k can be any position except 1 (if k=1, best is first, only 1 hire).  
   - How: Harmonic series H_m ≈ ln(m) + 0.577. Here, it's the sum of reciprocals.  
   - Context: This gives the exact probability formula.

**Solution to 5.2-3: Expected value of the sum of n dice using indicator variables**

Step 0: Set up the problem.  
   - We have n fair 6-sided dice. Each shows 1-6 with P=1/6. Sum S = sum of all dice values. Find E[S].  
   - Why indicators? They break S into simple 0/1 flags for each possible value on each die.  
   - How: For die i and value v (1-6), I_{i,v} = 1 if die i shows v, else 0. Then S = sum_i sum_v v * I_{i,v}.  
   - Context: This models the sum as a weighted sum of indicators.

Step 1: Find E[I_{i,v}].  
   - Since fair die, P(die i shows v) = 1/6. By indicator lemma, E[I_{i,v}] = P = 1/6.  
   - Why? The lemma says E[indicator] = probability of the event.  
   - How: It's the same for every i and v.  
   - Context: Independent of other dice.

Step 2: Use linearity of expectation.  
   - E[S] = E[sum_i sum_v v * I_{i,v}] = sum_i sum_v v * E[I_{i,v}] = sum_i sum_v v * (1/6).  
   - Inner sum over v: (1/6)(1+2+3+4+5+6) = (1/6)*21 = 3.5 (expected value of one die).  
   - Outer sum: n * 3.5.  
   - Why linearity? It holds even with dependence (though here dice are independent).  
   - How: Just add up the expectations.  
   - Context: This gives the average sum without needing full probability distribution.

**Solution to 5.2-4: Linearity of expectation with dependent dice**

Step 0: Understand the scenarios.  
   - Two 6-sided dice, each with values 1-6, P=1/6. Find E[sum] in different dependence cases.  
   - Why? To show linearity holds even when dependent.  
   - How: Calculate E[sum] directly in each case.  
   - Context: Demonstrates a key property of expectation.

Step 1: Scenario A: Independent dice.  
   - Each has E=3.5. Sum = X+Y, E[sum] = 3.5 + 3.5 = 7.  
   - Why? Independence doesn't matter for linearity.  
   - How: Standard calculation.

Step 2: Scenario B: Second die copies first.  
   - Let X = first die. Second = X. Sum = X + X = 2X. E[sum] = 2 * E[X] = 2*3.5 = 7.  
   - Why? Even fully dependent, expectation is the same.  
   - How: E[2X] = 2E[X].  
   - Context: Dependence doesn't break linearity.

Step 3: Scenario C: Second = 7 - first.  
   - X = first. Second = 7 - X. Sum = X + (7 - X) = 7 (constant). E[sum] = 7.  
   - Why? Perfect negative dependence, but E[X] + E[7-X] = 3.5 + (7-3.5) = 7.  
   - How: Linearity applies: E[sum] = E[X] + E[7-X].  
   - Context: Shows linearity's power in dependent cases.

**Solution to 5.2-5: Hat-check problem using indicator variables**

Step 0: Set up the problem.  
   - n customers give hats to a clerk, who returns them in uniform random order (each permutation equally likely).  
   - Find expected number of customers who get their own hat back (X = number of correct matches).  
   - Why indicators? X = sum of indicators for each customer getting their hat.  
   - How: I_i = 1 if customer i gets own hat, else 0. X = sum I_i.  
   - Context: Classic problem showing average matches in random assignment.

Step 1: Find E[I_i].  
   - In random permutation, any hat can go to any customer. P(customer i gets own hat) = 1/n (n equally likely positions for their hat).  
   - So, E[I_i] = 1/n (by indicator lemma).  
   - Why? Symmetric for each customer.  
   - How: There are n! permutations, and in exactly n! / n = (n-1)! of them, customer i gets their hat (fix i's hat, permute the rest).  
   - Context: Each customer is equally likely to get any hat.

Step 2: Use linearity.  
   - E[X] = sum E[I_i] = n * (1/n) = 1.  
   - Why? Linearity even though indicators are dependent (if i gets their hat, it affects others).  
   - How: Just sum the individual expectations.

**SIMPLIFIED EXPLANATIONS (Added for Clarity)**

### Quick Reminder of Basics (Simplified)
- **Probability**: Just a number (0 to 1) saying "how likely?" Example: Flipping a fair coin for heads is 0.5 (50% chance).
- **Expectation (Average)**: If you do something many times, what's the typical result? Example: Rolling a die many times, average value is 3.5.
- **Indicator Variables**: Like a yes/no flag. 1 if something happens, 0 if not. Their average is the probability.
- **Linearity**: For averages, E[A + B] = E[A] + E[B], even if A and B are related (e.g., two dice where one copies the other).
- **Permutations**: Ways to arrange items. For n items, there are n! (n factorial) ways, each equally likely in random order.

Now, let's walk through each 5.2 problem simply, step by step. I'll explain why each step matters and how it connects.

### 5.2-1: Probability of Hiring Exactly Once or Exactly n Times
**What it's asking**: In a hiring process, candidates come in random order. You hire the first one always. Then, you hire later ones only if they're better than everyone before. What's the chance you hire just once (best case) or exactly n times (worst case)?

**Simple Explanation**:
- Imagine n people lining up randomly for interviews. You always hire the first person you see.
- You hire a later person only if they're the best so far (better than all before them).
- For exactly 1 hire: This only happens if the absolute best person is first. Since the order is random, any of the n people could be first—each has a 1/n chance. So, probability = 1/n.
  - Why? Think of it like picking a random person to be first in line—only if that person is the very best do you stop after one hire.
- For exactly n hires: This means every person is better than all before them (like they're in perfect increasing order: person 1 is best of 1, person 2 is best of 2, etc.).
  - There's only 1 way this can happen out of n! possible orders (the "sorted" order). So, probability = 1/n!.
  - Why? Most orders aren't perfectly increasing, so this is rare for large n.

**Why does this matter?** It shows the range of possible outcomes in a random process.

### 5.2-2: Probability of Hiring Exactly Twice
**What it's asking**: Same setup—chance you hire exactly twice?

**Simple Explanation**:
- You always hire the first person.
- For a second hire, someone later must be better than everyone before them.
- But for exactly two, that second hire must be the overall best (no one after can be even better, or you'd hire a third time).
- Pick where the best person is (position k, from 2 to n). Chance = 1/n.
- Among the first k-1 people, the first one must be the best of those (otherwise, you'd hire someone else before k).
  - If there are m people, chance the first is the best = 1/m.
- Add up over all k: (1/n) × (1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/(n-1)) = (H_{n-1})/n.
  - H is like a sum of fractions (1 + 0.5 + 0.333 + ...). It's about ln(n) for big n.
- Why? We're counting all possible spots for the best person, with the condition on early hires.

**Why does this matter?** It builds on the previous by adding conditions.

### 5.2-3: Expected Sum of n Dice Using Indicators
**What it's asking**: Roll n fair dice (1-6 each). What's the average total?

**Simple Explanation**:
- Each die is independent. Average for one die: (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5.
- For n dice, average sum should be n × 3.5, right? But let's prove it with indicators.
- For each die i and each number v (1-6), make an indicator I_{i,v} = 1 if die i shows v, else 0.
- The sum S = sum over all i and v of (v × I_{i,v}).
- Average of each I_{i,v} = probability it shows v = 1/6.
- By linearity, average S = sum over i,v of v × (1/6) = n × (3.5).
- Why? Indicators break the sum into tiny yes/no parts, and their averages add up easily.

**Why does this matter?** Shows how to handle sums with averages, even without full probabilities.

### 5.2-4: Linearity with Dependent Dice
**What it's asking**: Two dice, but sometimes one depends on the other. What's the average sum each time?

**Simple Explanation**:
- Normal case: Two independent dice, each averages 3.5, sum averages 7.
- Case B: Second die copies the first. Sum = 2 × first die. Average = 2 × 3.5 = 7.
- Case C: Second = 7 - first. Sum = first + (7 - first) = 7 always! Average = 7.
- Why same answer? Linearity says E[A + B] = E[A] + E[B], even if A and B are linked (dependent). It doesn't care about the relationship—just adds averages.

**Why does this matter?** Proves linearity works no matter how things are connected.

### 5.2-5: Hat-Check Problem
**What it's asking**: n people give hats to a clerk, who returns them randomly. Average number who get their own hat back?

**Simple Explanation**:
- Random order means any hat can go to any person.
- For each person i, indicator I_i = 1 if they get their own hat, else 0.
- Chance for each = 1/n (n possible hats).
- Average for each I_i = 1/n.
- Total average matches = sum of averages = n × (1/n) = 1.
- Why? Even if hats are mixed, on average, exactly 1 person gets theirs back. (Though in reality, it could be 0, 1, 2, etc.)

**Why does this matter?** Counterintuitive result—shows averages can be simple even in complex setups.
