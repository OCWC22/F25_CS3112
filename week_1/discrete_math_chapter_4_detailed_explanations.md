### Example 4.1.1: Sum of Two Even Integers
Prove that the sum of any two even integers is even.

**Solution:**
Begin by asking yourself, "Where am I starting from?" and "What do I need to show?" To help answer these questions, introduce variables to represent the quantities in the statement to be proved.

Formal Restatement: ∀ even integers m and n, m + n is even.

**How to Read This Out Loud and What It Means (Step-by-Step Explanation):**
- **Start by saying the words:** "Formal Restatement: For all even integers m and n, m plus n is even."
- **Breaking it down slowly, without assuming any math knowledge:**
  - **"∀" (the upside-down A symbol):** This is a symbol called "universal quantifier" or "for all." It means "for every" or "for all possible." So, when you see "∀ even integers m and n," you read it as "for all even integers called m and n." It's like saying "no matter which even integers you pick, this will be true."
  - **"even integers m and n":** "Even integers" are numbers like 2, 4, 6, 8, etc.—they're divisible by 2. "m" and "n" are just placeholders, like variables representing any even integers. We don't assume you know this; we're introducing it step by step.
  - **"," (comma):** Just a separator, like a pause in speech.
  - **"m + n":** "m plus n" means m added to n. For example, if m is 4 and n is 6, m + n is 10.
  - **"is even":** This means the result is divisible by 2, like the even integers we started with.
- **Putting it all together in plain English:** This whole thing is a fancy way of saying: "No matter which two even integers you pick (call them m and n), when you add them together, the result will always be even."
- **Why do we use this fancy notation?** It's a shortcut to write precise statements without words. In math, we use symbols to be clear and avoid repetition. This is like a universal rule we're trying to prove.
- **Assumptions and considerations:** We're assuming m and n are both even (as stated). We don't assume anything about their sum yet—we have to prove it. The thought process is: Start with any two even integers m and n, and show that their sum is even.

### Example 4.1.3: Difference of Integers with Same Parity
Prove that for all integers m and n, if m and n are both odd or both even, then m - n is even.

**Solution:**
Begin by asking yourself, "Where am I starting from?" and "What do I need to show?" To help answer these questions, introduce variables to represent the quantities in the statement to be proved.

Formal Restatement: ∀ integers m and n, if (m and n are both odd) ∨ (m and n are both even) then m - n is even.

**How to Read This Out Loud and What It Means (Step-by-Step Explanation):**
- **Start by saying the words:** "Formal Restatement: For all integers m and n, if m and n are both odd or m and n are both even, then m minus n is even."
- **Breaking it down slowly, without assuming any math knowledge:**
  - **"∀" (the upside-down A symbol):** This is a symbol called "universal quantifier" or "for all." It means "for every" or "for all possible." So, when you see "∀ integers m and n," you read it as "for all integers called m and n." It's like saying "no matter which integers you pick, this will be true."
  - **"integers m and n":** "Integers" are whole numbers, like 1, 2, 3, or -1, -2, -3. "m" and "n" are just placeholders, like variables representing any integers. We don't assume you know this; we're introducing it step by step.
  - **"," (comma):** Just a separator, like a pause in speech.
  - **"if":** This indicates a condition or hypothesis that must be true.
  - **"(" and ")":** These are grouping symbols, like parentheses in regular text.
  - **"∨" (the V symbol):** This is the "or" symbol in logic. It means "either...or...or both."
  - **"m and n are both odd":** This means both numbers have the same parity - both are odd numbers.
  - **"m and n are both even":** This means both numbers have the same parity - both are even numbers.
  - **"then":** This indicates what follows if the condition is true.
  - **"m - n":** "m minus n" means m subtracted by n. For example, if m is 7 and n is 3, m - n is 4.
  - **"is even":** This means the result is divisible by 2.
- **Putting it all together in plain English:** This whole thing is a fancy way of saying: "No matter which two integers you pick (call them m and n), if they have the same parity (both odd or both even), then when you subtract one from the other, the result will always be even."
- **Why do we use this fancy notation?** It's a shortcut to write precise statements without words. In math, we use symbols to be clear and avoid repetition. This is like a conditional rule we're trying to prove.

### Example 4.2.1: Determining Whether Numbers Are Rational or Irrational
Determine which of the following numbers are rational and which are irrational:
a. Is 10/3 a rational number?
b. Is -5/39 a rational number?
c. Is 0.281 a rational number?
d. Is 7 a rational number?
e. Is 0 a rational number?
f. Is 2/0 a rational number?
g. Is 2/0 an irrational number?
h. Is 0.12121212... a rational number (where the digits 12 are assumed to repeat forever)?
i. If m and n are integers and neither m nor n is zero, is (m + n)/mn a rational number?

**Solution:**
Begin by asking yourself, "Where am I starting from?" and "What do I need to show?" To help answer these questions, introduce variables to represent the quantities in the statement to be proved.

Formal Restatement: Determine for each given number whether it is rational or irrational, where a rational number is defined as a real number that can be expressed as a/b for integers a, b with b ≠ 0.

**How to Read This Out Loud and What It Means (Step-by-Step Explanation):**
- **Start by saying the words:** "Formal Restatement: Determine for each given number whether it is rational or irrational, where a rational number is defined as a real number that can be expressed as a over b for integers a, b with b not equal to zero."
- **Breaking it down slowly, without assuming any math knowledge:**
  - **"Determine":** This means to figure out or decide.
  - **"for each given number":** We need to analyze each number in the list separately.
  - **"whether it is rational or irrational":** We need to classify each number as either rational (can be written as a fraction) or irrational (cannot be written as a simple fraction).
  - **"where a rational number is defined as":** This gives us the definition we're using.
  - **"a real number":** Any number on the number line, including fractions, decimals, and square roots.
  - **"that can be expressed as":** That can be written in the form.
  - **"a/b":** "a over b" or "a divided by b", where a and b are integers.
  - **"for integers a, b":** a and b are whole numbers (positive, negative, or zero).
  - **"with b ≠ 0":** b cannot be zero (division by zero is undefined).
- **Putting it all together in plain English:** This is asking us to look at each number and determine if it can be written as a fraction (rational) or if it cannot be written as a simple fraction (irrational).
- **Why do we use this fancy notation?** It gives us a precise definition to work with, so we can consistently classify numbers.
- **Assumptions and considerations:** We need to recall the definition of rational numbers and apply it to each example. The thought process is: For each number, check if it can be expressed as a fraction with integer numerator and denominator.

  - **"n = m·k":** n equals m multiplied by k.

  - **"n²":** "n squared" means n multiplied by itself. For example, if n is 3, n² is 9. The superscript 2 means "to the power of 2."
  - **"=" (equals sign):** Means "is equal to" or "equals."
  - **"8m":** 8 times m. If m is 2, this is 16.
  - **"+" (plus sign):** Means addition.
  - **"1" (the number):** Just the number one.
- **Putting it all together in plain English:** This whole thing is a fancy way of saying: "No matter which odd integer you pick (call it n), you can always find some whole number (call it m) so that when you square n, it equals 8 times m plus 1."
- **Why do we use this fancy notation?** It's a shortcut to write precise statements without words. In math, we use symbols to be clear and avoid repetition. This is like a universal rule we're trying to prove.
