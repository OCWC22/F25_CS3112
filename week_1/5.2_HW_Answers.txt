### Problem 1
**Use mathematical induction to show that any amount of money of at least 14¢ can be made up using 3¢ and 8¢ coins.**

Let P(n) be the statement "n cents can be made using 3¢ and 8¢ coins." We want to prove P(n) is true for all integers n >= 14.

**Basis Step (n=14):**
We must show that P(14) is true.
14¢ can be made with two 3¢ coins and one 8¢ coin: 2 * 3 + 1 * 8 = 6 + 8 = 14.
Thus, the basis step is true.

**Inductive Step:**
1.  **Inductive Hypothesis:** Suppose that for some integer k >= 14, P(k) is true. That is, k cents can be made using 3¢ and 8¢ coins.

2.  **Goal:** We must show that P(k+1) is true. That is, k+1 cents can be made using 3¢ and 8¢ coins.

3.  **Proof:**
    Starting with the k cents from the inductive hypothesis, we consider two cases for how the k cents are formed:

    *   **Case 1: The combination for k cents includes at least one 8¢ coin.**
        To make k+1 cents, we can replace one 8¢ coin with three 3¢ coins. This changes the total value by: -8 + 3*(3) = -8 + 9 = +1 cent.
        So, if k cents can be made with at least one 8¢ coin, k+1 cents can also be made.

    *   **Case 2: The combination for k cents uses no 8¢ coins.**
        In this case, the k cents must be made up entirely of 3¢ coins. So, k = 3m for some integer m.
        Since we know k >= 14, it must be that 3m >= 14, which means m >= 14/3, so m must be at least 5.
        This means there are at least five 3¢ coins in the combination for k.
        To make k+1 cents, we can replace five 3¢ coins (15¢) with two 8¢ coins (16¢). This changes the total value by: -5*(3) + 2*(8) = -15 + 16 = +1 cent.
        So, if k cents can be made with only 3¢ coins, k+1 cents can also be made.

Since both possible cases lead to the conclusion that k+1 cents can be formed, the inductive step is true.

**Conclusion:**
Because the basis step and the inductive step have been proved, by the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all integers n >= 14.

### Problem 7
**Prove that 1 + 6 + 11 + 16 + ... + (5n - 4) = n(5n - 3)/2 for all integers n >= 1.**

Let P(n) be the statement 1 + 6 + 11 + ... + (5n - 4) = n(5n - 3)/2.

**Basis Step (n=1):**
LHS = 1
RHS = 1(5*1 - 3)/2 = 1(2)/2 = 1
Since LHS = RHS, P(1) is true.

**Inductive Step:**
1.  **Inductive Hypothesis:** Suppose P(k) is true for some integer k >= 1.
    That is, 1 + 6 + 11 + ... + (5k - 4) = k(5k - 3)/2.

2.  **Goal:** We must show P(k+1) is true.
    That is, 1 + 6 + ... + (5(k+1) - 4) = (k+1)(5(k+1) - 3)/2.
    The RHS simplifies to (k+1)(5k + 2)/2.

3.  **Proof:**
    LHS of P(k+1) = [1 + 6 + ... + (5k - 4)] + (5(k+1) - 4)
    By the inductive hypothesis, we substitute for the bracketed part:
    = [k(5k - 3)/2] + (5k + 5 - 4)
    = [k(5k - 3)/2] + (5k + 1)
    Find a common denominator:
    = (5k² - 3k)/2 + (2(5k + 1))/2
    = (5k² - 3k + 10k + 2)/2
    = (5k² + 7k + 2)/2
    Factor the numerator:
    = (k+1)(5k+2)/2
    This is the simplified RHS of P(k+1).

**Conclusion:**
Because the basis step and the inductive step have been proved, by the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all integers n >= 1.

### Problem 9

RHS = 4(4³ - 16)/3 = 4(64 - 16)/3 = 4(48)/3 = 4 * 16 = 64

    That is, 4³ + 4⁴ + ... + 4ᵏ = 4(4ᵏ - 16)/3.

2.  **Goal:** We must show P(k+1) is true.
    That is, 4³ + 4⁴ + ... + 4ᵏ⁺¹ = 4(4ᵏ⁺¹ - 16)/3.

3.  **Proof:**
    LHS of P(k+1) = [4³ + 4⁴ + ... + 4ᵏ] + 4ᵏ⁺¹
    By the inductive hypothesis:
    = [4(4ᵏ - 16)/3] + 4ᵏ⁺¹
    = (4 * 4ᵏ - 64)/3 + 4ᵏ⁺¹
    = (4ᵏ⁺¹ - 64)/3 + (3 * 4ᵏ⁺¹)/3
    = (4ᵏ⁺¹ - 64 + 3 * 4ᵏ⁺¹)/3
    = (4 * 4ᵏ⁺¹ - 64)/3
    = (4 * (4ᵏ⁺¹ - 16))/3
    = 4(4ᵏ⁺¹ - 16)/3
    This is the RHS of P(k+1).

**Conclusion:**
Because the basis step and the inductive step have been proved, by the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all integers n >= 3.

### Problem 13
**Prove that ∑(from i=1 to n-1) i(i+1) = n(n-1)(n+1)/3 for all integers n >= 2.**

Let P(n) be the statement ∑(from i=1 to n-1) i(i+1) = n(n-1)(n+1)/3.

**Basis Step (n=2):**
LHS = ∑(from i=1 to 1) i(i+1) = 1(1+1) = 2
RHS = 2(2-1)(2+1)/3 = 2(1)(3)/3 = 2
Since LHS = RHS, P(2) is true.

**Inductive Step:**
1.  **Inductive Hypothesis:** Suppose P(k) is true for some integer k >= 2.
    That is, ∑(from i=1 to k-1) i(i+1) = k(k-1)(k+1)/3.

2.  **Goal:** We must show P(k+1) is true.
    That is, ∑(from i=1 to k) i(i+1) = (k+1)((k+1)-1)((k+1)+1)/3 = (k+1)k(k+2)/3.

3.  **Proof:**
    LHS of P(k+1) = ∑(from i=1 to k) i(i+1)
    Separate the final term:
    = [∑(from i=1 to k-1) i(i+1)] + k(k+1)
    By the inductive hypothesis:
    = [k(k-1)(k+1)/3] + k(k+1)
    Factor out k(k+1):
    = k(k+1) * [(k-1)/3 + 1]
    Find a common denominator in the brackets:
    = k(k+1) * [(k-1 + 3)/3]
    = k(k+1) * [(k+2)/3]
    = k(k+1)(k+2)/3
    This is the RHS of P(k+1).

**Conclusion:**
Because the basis step and the inductive step have been proved, by the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all integers n >= 2.

### Problem 15
**Prove that ∑(from i=1 to n) i(i!) = (n+1)! - 1 for all integers n >= 1.**

Let P(n) be the statement ∑(from i=1 to n) i(i!) = (n+1)! - 1.

**Basis Step (n=1):**
LHS = ∑(from i=1 to 1) i(i!) = 1(1!) = 1
RHS = (1+1)! - 1 = 2! - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1
Since LHS = RHS, P(1) is true.

**Inductive Step:**
1.  **Inductive Hypothesis:** Suppose P(k) is true for some integer k >= 1.
    That is, ∑(from i=1 to k) i(i!) = (k+1)! - 1.

2.  **Goal:** We must show P(k+1) is true.
    That is, ∑(from i=1 to k+1) i(i!) = ((k+1)+1)! - 1 = (k+2)! - 1.

3.  **Proof:**
    LHS of P(k+1) = ∑(from i=1 to k+1) i(i!)
    Separate the final term:
    = [∑(from i=1 to k) i(i!)] + (k+1)((k+1)!)
    By the inductive hypothesis:
    = [(k+1)! - 1] + (k+1)((k+1)!)
    Group the factorial terms:
    = (k+1)! + (k+1)((k+1)!) - 1
    Factor out (k+1)!:
    = (k+1)! * (1 + (k+1)) - 1
    = (k+1)! * (k+2) - 1
    By definition of factorial, (k+2)(k+1)! = (k+2)!:
    = (k+2)! - 1
    This is the RHS of P(k+1).

**Conclusion:**
Because the basis step and the inductive step have been proved, by the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all integers n >= 1.

### Problem 19
**Prove that for any integer n >= 1, if x is a real number, then d/dx (xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹.**

Let P(n) be the statement d/dx (xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹.

**Basis Step (n=1):**
LHS = d/dx (x¹) = 1
RHS = 1 * x¹⁻¹ = 1 * x⁰ = 1
Since LHS = RHS, P(1) is true.

**Inductive Step:**
1.  **Inductive Hypothesis:** Suppose P(k) is true for some integer k >= 1.
    That is, d/dx (xᵏ) = kxᵏ⁻¹.

2.  **Goal:** We must show P(k+1) is true.
    That is, d/dx (xᵏ⁺¹) = (k+1)xᵏ.

3.  **Proof:**
    LHS of P(k+1) = d/dx (xᵏ⁺¹)
    Rewrite xᵏ⁺¹ as a product:
    = d/dx (x * xᵏ)
    Using the product rule for derivatives, (fg)' = f'g + fg':
    = (d/dx(x) * xᵏ) + (x * d/dx(xᵏ))
    = (1 * xᵏ) + (x * (kxᵏ⁻¹))  (by the inductive hypothesis)
    = xᵏ + kxᵏ
    = (1+k)xᵏ
    = (k+1)xᵏ
    This is the RHS of P(k+1).

**Conclusion:**
Because the basis step and the inductive step have been proved, by the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all integers n >= 1.

### Problem 33 (Find the mistake in the proof)
**Theorem:** For any integer n ≥ 1, 1² + 2² + ... + n² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6.

**Analysis of the Proof:**
The proof attempts to use mathematical induction.

1.  **Basis Step:** The proof correctly shows that for n=1, the left-hand side (1²) and the right-hand side (1(2)(3)/6) both equal 1. This part is correct.

2.  **Inductive Step:** The proof then states: "Suppose that for some integer k ≥ 1, k² = k(k+1)(2k+1)/6."

**The Mistake:**
The mistake is in the formulation of the **inductive hypothesis**. The inductive hypothesis, P(k), should assume that the entire formula is true for the value k, not just for a single term.

*   **Incorrect Inductive Hypothesis (as written in the proof):**
    `k² = k(k+1)(2k+1)/6`
    This statement is fundamentally wrong. It claims that the last term of the series is equal to the formula for the entire sum.

*   **Correct Inductive Hypothesis (what it should have been):**
    `1² + 2² + ... + k² = k(k+1)(2k+1)/6`
    This correctly states that we assume the entire sum up to k is equal to the formula.

Because the proof starts the inductive step with a false assumption, the rest of the reasoning in that step is invalid.

### Problem 35 (Find the mistake in the proof)
**Theorem:** For any integer n ≥ 1, ∑(from i=1 to n) i(i!) = (n+1)! - 1.

**Analysis of the Proof:**
The proof attempts to show the basis step (P(1)) is true.

The proof states: "When n=1, ∑(from i=1 to 1) i(i!) = (1+1)! - 1. So 1(1!) = 2! - 1 and 1 = 1."

**The Mistake:**
The mistake is in the **logical flow of the basis step**. The proof uses circular reasoning. It begins by *assuming* that the left-hand side equals the right-hand side for n=1, rather than calculating each side independently and then showing they are equal.

*   **Incorrect Logic (as written in the proof):**
    It starts with the statement `LHS = RHS` for n=1 and works from there. This assumes the very thing it is supposed to be proving.

*   **Correct Logic (what it should have been):**
    1.  **Evaluate the Left-Hand Side (LHS) for n=1:**
        LHS = ∑(from i=1 to 1) i(i!) = 1(1!) = 1.
    2.  **Evaluate the Right-Hand Side (RHS) for n=1:**
        RHS = (1+1)! - 1 = 2! - 1 = 2 - 1 = 1.
    3.  **Conclusion:** Since LHS = RHS (1 = 1), the basis step is true.

The provided proof is also incomplete, as it never proceeds to the inductive step. The primary error is the flawed reasoning in the basis step.
