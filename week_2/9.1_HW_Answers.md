## Exercise 1: Tossing Two Coins 50 Times
**Problem:** Toss two coins 50 times and make a table showing the relative frequencies of 0, 1, and 2 heads. How do your values compare with the theoretical expectations?

**Solution:**

**1. Theoretical Probability Analysis:**
First, let's determine the theoretical probabilities for tossing two fair coins. The set of all possible outcomes (the sample space) is:
- **HH**: Two heads
- **HT**: One head, one tail
- **TH**: One tail, one head
- **TT**: Two tails

There are 4 equally likely outcomes.

- **Probability of 2 heads (HH):** There is only 1 outcome with two heads.
  `P(2 heads) = 1/4 = 0.25` or 25%.
- **Probability of 1 head (HT or TH):** There are 2 outcomes with one head.
  `P(1 head) = 2/4 = 1/2 = 0.50` or 50%.
- **Probability of 0 heads (TT):** There is only 1 outcome with zero heads.
  `P(0 heads) = 1/4 = 0.25` or 25%.

**2. Expected Frequencies for 50 Tosses:**
Now, we can calculate the expected number of times each result would appear in 50 tosses:
- **Expected 2 heads:** `50 * P(2 heads) = 50 * 0.25 = 12.5` times.
- **Expected 1 head:** `50 * P(1 head) = 50 * 0.50 = 25` times.
- **Expected 0 heads:** `50 * P(0 heads) = 50 * 0.25 = 12.5` times.

**3. Experimental Data and Relative Frequencies (from Table 9.1.1):**
The problem provides the following experimental results from 50 tosses:
- **2 heads obtained:** 11 times.
  Relative Frequency = `11 / 50 = 0.22` or 22%.
- **1 head obtained:** 27 times.
  Relative Frequency = `27 / 50 = 0.54` or 54%.
- **0 heads obtained:** 12 times.
  Relative Frequency = `12 / 50 = 0.24` or 24%.

**4. Comparison and Analysis:**
Let's compare the experimental results to our theoretical expectations in a table:

| # of Heads | Theoretical P. | Expected in 50 | Experimental | Relative Freq. |
|------------|----------------|----------------|--------------|----------------|
| 2          | 25%            | 12.5           | 11           | 22%            |
| 1          | 50%            | 25             | 27           | 54%            |
| 0          | 25%            | 12.5           | 12           | 24%            |

**Conclusion:** The experimental results are very close to the theoretical predictions. The small differences are expected due to the randomness inherent in a real-world experiment. The Law of Large Numbers suggests that if we were to conduct many more tosses, the experimental relative frequencies would likely get even closer to the theoretical probabilities.

## Exercise 3: Red card that is not a face card
**Problem:** The event that the chosen card is red and is not a face card.

**Solution:**

**1. Define the Sample Space:**
The sample space is a standard 52-card deck.

**2. Identify Favorable Outcomes:**
We need a card that meets two conditions: it must be red, and it must not be a face card.
- **Red Cards:** There are two red suits: Hearts (♥) and Diamonds (♦). Each suit has 13 cards, so there are `13 + 13 = 26` red cards.
- **Face Cards:** These are the Jack (J), Queen (Q), and King (K). There are 3 face cards per suit.
- **Red Face Cards:** There are 3 face cards in Hearts and 3 in Diamonds, for a total of `3 + 3 = 6` red face cards.
- **Red Non-Face Cards:** To find the number of red cards that are *not* face cards, we subtract the red face cards from the total number of red cards: `26 - 6 = 20`.

**3. Calculate the Probability:**
The probability of an event is the number of favorable outcomes divided by the total number of possible outcomes.
- **Favorable Outcomes:** 20 (red non-face cards)
- **Total Outcomes:** 52 (total cards in the deck)

`P(Red and Not Face Card) = 20 / 52`

**4. Simplify the Fraction:**
`20 / 52 = 5 / 13`

## Exercise 5: Denomination at least 10 (aces high)
**Problem:** The event that the denomination of the chosen card is at least 10 (counting aces high).

**Solution:**

**1. Define the Sample Space:**
The sample space is a standard 52-card deck.

**2. Identify Favorable Denominations:**
"At least 10" means the card's value must be 10 or greater. Counting aces as high, the favorable denominations are:
- 10
- Jack (J)
- Queen (Q)
- King (K)
- Ace (A)
This gives us 5 favorable denominations.

**3. Count the Favorable Cards:**
Each of these 5 denominations appears in all 4 suits (Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs, Spades).
- **Total Favorable Cards:** `5 denominations * 4 suits = 20` cards.

**4. Calculate the Probability:**
`P(Denomination ≥ 10) = (Favorable Cards) / (Total Cards) = 20 / 52`

**5. Simplify the Fraction:**
`20 / 52 = 5 / 13`

- (2, 6)

## Exercise 9: Sum at most 6

The total number of outcomes is 36.

**2. Identify Favorable Outcomes:**

- **Sum = 2:** (1, 1) - (1 outcome)

**4. Calculate the Probability:**
`P(Sum ≤ 6) = (Favorable Outcomes) / (Total Outcomes) = 15 / 36`

## Exercise 11: Three Coin Tosses
**Problem:** Suppose that a coin is tossed three times. Find the probabilities for (i) exactly one head, (ii) at least two heads, and (iii) no heads.

**Solution:**

**1. Define the Sample Space:**
For three coin tosses, there are `2^3 = 8` possible outcomes:
{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT}

**2. Calculate Probabilities:**

**(i) Exactly one head:**
- Favorable outcomes: {HTT, THT, TTH}
- Number of favorable outcomes = 3
- `P(Exactly one head) = 3 / 8`

**(ii) At least two heads:**
- This means 2 or 3 heads.
- Favorable outcomes: {HHT, HTH, THH, HHH}
- Number of favorable outcomes = 4
- `P(At least two heads) = 4 / 8 = 1/2`

**(iii) No heads:**
- Favorable outcome: {TTT}
- Number of favorable outcomes = 1
- `P(No heads) = 1 / 8`

## Exercise 13: True/False Exam

**Solution:**

This problem is mathematically identical to the 'Three Coin Tosses' problem. Each question has two possible outcomes (Correct/Wrong), just like a coin has two outcomes (Heads/Tails). There are three independent trials (questions).

**2. Define the Sample Space:**
Let C = Correct, W = Wrong. The 8 outcomes are:
{CCC, CCW, CWC, CWW, WCC, WCW, WWC, WWW}

**3. Calculate Probabilities:**

**(i) Exactly one correct:**
- Favorable outcomes: {CWW, WCW, WWC}
- `P(Exactly one correct) = 3 / 8`

**(ii) At least two correct:**
- Favorable outcomes: {CCW, CWC, WCC, CCC}
- `P(At least two correct) = 4 / 8 = 1/2`

**(iii) No answers correct:**
- Favorable outcome: {WWW}
- `P(No answers correct) = 1 / 8`

## Exercise 15: Relationship Between Exercises
**Problem:** Comment on the relationship between your answer to exercise 11 and your answers to exercises 12-14.

**Solution:**
Exercises 11 (coin tosses), 12 (children's gender), 13 (exam answers), and 14 (illness exposure) are all examples of a **Bernoulli process** with 3 trials. They share the same underlying mathematical structure:
- Each problem consists of a fixed number of independent trials (n=3).
- Each trial has exactly two possible outcomes (e.g., Heads/Tails, Boy/Girl, Correct/Wrong).
- The probability of success (p) is the same for each trial (p=0.5 in these cases).

This shared structure results in an identical sample space of `2^3 = 8` equally likely outcomes. Consequently, the probabilities for events like 'exactly one success', 'at least two successes', or 'no successes' are the same (3/8, 4/8, and 1/8, respectively) across all four scenarios. This illustrates a core principle of probability: abstract mathematical models can describe a wide variety of seemingly unrelated real-world phenomena.

## Exercise 17: Continuation of Exercise 16
**Problem:** A six-sided die has two faces painted red (R), two blue (B), and two yellow (Y). If the die is rolled three times, find the probability that exactly one of the colors that appears is red.

**Solution:**

**1. Define the Sample Space:**
For each roll, there are 3 possible color outcomes (R, B, Y), each with a probability of 1/3. For three rolls, the total number of equally likely outcomes is `3 * 3 * 3 = 27`.

**2. Identify Favorable Outcomes for 'Exactly One Red':**
This means one roll is Red, and the other two are Not Red (i.e., Blue or Yellow).

- **Step 1: Choose the position for the Red roll.** The red can occur on the 1st, 2nd, or 3rd roll. (3 choices)
- **Step 2: Determine the outcomes for the other two rolls.** Each of these two rolls must be non-red, so they can be either B or Y. (2 choices for each roll).

Let's list the possibilities:
- **R on 1st roll:** RBY, RYB, RBB, RYY (4 outcomes)
- **R on 2nd roll:** BRY, YRB, BBR, YYR (4 outcomes)
- **R on 3rd roll:** BYR, YBR, BBY, YYB (4 outcomes)
Wait, let's use the multiplication principle for a cleaner approach.
- Choose which of the 3 rolls is Red: `C(3,1) = 3` ways.
- For the other 2 rolls, each can be B or Y: `2 * 2 = 4` ways.
- **Total favorable outcomes:** `3 * 4 = 12`.

**3. Calculate the Probability:**
`P(Exactly one Red) = (Favorable Outcomes) / (Total Outcomes) = 12 / 27`

**4. Simplify the Fraction:**
`12 / 27 = 4 / 9`

## Exercise 19: Urn with Two Blue and Three White Balls
**Problem:** An urn contains two blue balls and three white balls. One ball is drawn, its color is recorded, and it is replaced. Then another ball is drawn. Find the probabilities of drawing (a) both blue, (b) both white, (c) same color, (d) different colors.

**Solution:**

**1. Define the Probabilities for a Single Draw:**
Total balls = 2 Blue + 3 White = 5 balls.
- `P(Blue) = 2/5`
- `P(White) = 3/5`
Since the ball is replaced, the draws are independent events.

**2. Calculate Probabilities for Two Draws:**

**(a) Both balls are blue:**
`P(Blue and Blue) = P(Blue) * P(Blue) = (2/5) * (2/5) = 4/25`

**(b) Both balls are white:**
`P(White and White) = P(White) * P(White) = (3/5) * (3/5) = 9/25`

**(c) The two balls are the same color:**
This means they are either both blue OR both white. Since these are mutually exclusive events, we add their probabilities.
`P(Same Color) = P(Both Blue) + P(Both White) = 4/25 + 9/25 = 13/25`

**(d) The two balls are different colors:**
This is the complement of drawing the same color.
`P(Different Colors) = 1 - P(Same Color) = 1 - 13/25 = 12/25`

## Exercise 21: Two-Digit Multiples of 3
**Problem:** a. How many positive two-digit integers are multiples of 3? b. What is the probability that a randomly chosen positive two-digit integer is a multiple of 3?

**Solution:**

**Part (a): Count the Multiples of 3**

**1. Identify the Range:**
Positive two-digit integers range from 10 to 99, inclusive.

**2. Find the First and Last Multiple in the Range:**
- Smallest multiple of 3 ≥ 10 is `3 * 4 = 12`.
- Largest multiple of 3 ≤ 99 is `3 * 33 = 99`.

**3. Count the Terms:**
The multiples form an arithmetic sequence: 12, 15, ..., 99. We can find the number of terms using the formula:
`Number of terms = ((Last - First) / Common Difference) + 1`
`Number of terms = ((99 - 12) / 3) + 1 = (87 / 3) + 1 = 29 + 1 = 30`.
There are **30** two-digit multiples of 3.

**Part (b): Calculate the Probability**

**1. Count the Total Number of Two-Digit Integers:**
`Total Integers = 99 - 10 + 1 = 90`.

**2. Calculate the Probability:**
`P(Multiple of 3) = (Favorable Outcomes) / (Total Outcomes) = 30 / 90 = 1/3`.

## Exercise 23: Array Probability (n ≥ 50)
**Problem:** Suppose A[1], A[2], ..., A[n] is a one-dimensional array and n ≥ 50.

**Solution:**

**(a) How many elements are in the array?**
- The number of elements is `n - 1 + 1 = n`.

**(b) How many elements are in the subarray A[4], ..., A[39]?**
- The number of elements is `39 - 4 + 1 = 36`.

**(c) Probability that a randomly chosen element is in A[3], ..., A[m]?**
- **Favorable outcomes (size of subarray):** `m - 3 + 1 = m - 2`.
- **Total outcomes (size of array):** `n`.
- **Probability:** `(m - 2) / n`.

**(d) Probability for n=39 in subarray A[⌊n/2⌋], ..., A[n]?**
- **Find the starting index:** `⌊39/2⌋ = ⌊19.5⌋ = 19`.
- **Subarray:** A[19], ..., A[39].
- **Favorable outcomes:** `39 - 19 + 1 = 21`.
- **Total outcomes:** 39.
- **Probability:** `21 / 39 = 7/13`.

## Exercise 25: Second Half of Array (n ≥ 2)
**Problem:** Consider the subarray A[⌈n/2⌉], ..., A[n]. Find the number of elements and the probability of choosing an element from it.

**Solution:**

**Case 1: n is even** (e.g., n=10)
- **Starting index:** `⌈n/2⌉ = n/2`. (For n=10, index is 5).
- **Subarray:** A[n/2], ..., A[n].
- **Number of elements:** `n - (n/2) + 1 = n/2 + 1`.
- **Probability:** `(n/2 + 1) / n`.

**Case 2: n is odd** (e.g., n=11)
- **Starting index:** `⌈n/2⌉ = (n+1)/2`. (For n=11, index is 6).
- **Subarray:** A[(n+1)/2], ..., A[n].
- **Number of elements:** `n - ((n+1)/2) + 1 = (2n - n - 1 + 2)/2 = (n+1)/2`.
- **Probability:** `((n+1)/2) / n = (n+1)/(2n)`.

**Problem:** What is the 62nd element in the one-dimensional array B[29], B[30], . . . , B[100]?

- 1st element is B[29]
- 2nd element is B[30] = B[29 + 1]

**2. Formulate the Rule:**

- Index = `29 + (62 - 1) = 29 + 61 = 90`.
- The 62nd element is **B[90]**.

## Exercise 29: Smallest of 87 Consecutive Integers with Largest 326
**Problem:** If the largest of 87 consecutive integers is 326, what is the smallest?

**Solution:**

**1. Understand the Relationship:**
For a set of `k` consecutive integers, the difference between the largest and smallest is `k - 1`.
`Largest - Smallest = k - 1`

**2. Rearrange the Formula to Solve for the Smallest:**
`Smallest = Largest - (k - 1)`

**3. Substitute the Given Values:**
- `Largest = 326`
- `k = 87`

`Smallest = 326 - (87 - 1) = 326 - 86 = 240`.

## Exercise 31: Multiples of 3 Between 1 and 1001
**Problem:** How many integers are there between 1 and 1,001 that are multiples of 3?

**Solution:**

**1. Identify the Range and Multiples:**
We are looking for multiples of 3 in the range [1, 1001].
- Smallest multiple of 3 in range: `3 * 1 = 3`.
- Largest multiple of 3 in range: `3 * 333 = 999`.

**2. Count the Multiples:**
The multiples form the sequence 3, 6, 9, ..., 999.

**Method 1: Using the Arithmetic Sequence Formula**
`Number of terms = ((Last - First) / Difference) + 1`
`Number of terms = ((999 - 3) / 3) + 1 = (996 / 3) + 1 = 332 + 1 = 333`.

**Method 2: Using Division**
Since the sequence starts at `3*1` and ends at `3*333`, the number of multiples is simply 333.
There are **333** multiples of 3 between 1 and 1001.
