## Exercise 1: Tossing Two Coins 30 Times

**Problem:** Toss two coins 30 times and make a table showing the relative frequencies of 0, 1, and 2 heads. How do your values compare with those shown in Table 9.1.1?

**Solution:**
This is a theoretical exercise, but we can analyze the expected outcomes based on probability theory.

**Theoretical Analysis:**
When tossing two coins, there are 4 equally likely outcomes:
- HH (2 heads)
- HT (1 head)
- TH (1 head)
- TT (0 heads)

**Expected frequencies for 30 tosses:**
- 2 heads: 30 × (1/4) = 7.5 times (25%)
- 1 head: 30 × (2/4) = 15 times (50%)
- 0 heads: 30 × (1/4) = 7.5 times (25%)

**Comparison with Table 9.1.1 (50 tosses):**
- Table 9.1.1 showed: 11 (22%), 27 (54%), 12 (24%)
- Our expected: 7.5 (25%), 15 (50%), 7.5 (25%)

The theoretical expectation shows slightly higher frequencies for 2 heads and 0 heads compared to the experimental data, which is expected due to random variation in small samples.

## Summary of Key Probability Concepts Covered

### 1. Sample Space and Events
- Sample space: Set of all possible outcomes
- Event: Subset of sample space
- Probability of event E: P(E) = N(E)/N(S) for equally likely outcomes

### 2. Equally Likely Probability Formula
P(E) = Number of favorable outcomes / Total number of outcomes

### 3. Complementary Events
P(E) + P(E') = 1, where E' is the complement of E

### 4. Addition Rule for Mutually Exclusive Events
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) if A and B are mutually exclusive

### 5. Multiplicative Rule for Independent Events
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) if A and B are independent

### 6. Conditional Probability
P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

## Additional Insights and Connections

### The Monty Hall Problem (Example 9.1.3) - Why It Matters

The Monty Hall problem demonstrates a counterintuitive result that highlights the importance of understanding conditional probability. The key insight is that the host's action of opening a door provides additional information that changes the probabilities.

**Why switching works:**
- Initially: P(prize behind A) = P(prize behind B) = P(prize behind C) = 1/3
- After host opens door C (revealing no prize): The probability redistributes
- P(prize behind A) remains 1/3
- P(prize behind B) becomes 2/3 (because the 1/3 that was originally behind C now goes to B)

### Practical Applications of These Concepts

1. **Medical Testing:** Understanding false positives/negatives (conditional probability)
2. **Quality Control:** Sampling and defect probabilities
3. **Risk Assessment:** Insurance and financial probability calculations
4. **Computer Science:** Algorithm analysis, hashing, network reliability
5. **Sports Statistics:** Player performance probabilities
6. **Weather Forecasting:** Probabilistic weather predictions

### Common Probability Fallacies to Avoid

1. **Gambler's Fallacy:** "If I've flipped heads 5 times in a row, tails is due next"
   - Each flip is independent; past outcomes don't affect future ones

2. **Confusion of Conditional Probability:** Forgetting that P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A)
   - Medical test example: P(disease|positive test) ≠ P(positive test|disease)

3. **Base Rate Fallacy:** Ignoring prior probabilities
   - Rare disease with 99% accurate test: Most positive results are false positives

- **Markov Chains:** Systems that transition between states probabilistically
- **Queueing Theory:** Modeling waiting lines and service systems

### Exercise 20: Monty Hall Problem with 5 Doors

**Problem:** Five doors (A, B, C, D, E) with prize behind one. You pick A. Host opens one empty door, then offers switch to another closed door.

**Solution:**
This is a generalization of the 3-door Monty Hall problem.

**Initial probabilities:** Each door has P = 1/5 = 0.2

**After you pick A and host opens one empty door:**
- The probability that prize is behind A remains 1/5
- The probability that prize is behind the remaining 3 unopened doors is 3/5 each

**a) Probability if you stick with original choice (door A):**
P(win by staying) = 1/5 = 0.2 or 20%

**b) Probability if you switch to another door:**
P(win by switching) = 3/5 = 0.6 or 60%

**Explanation:**
When the host opens an empty door, the probability mass from that door (1/5) gets redistributed among the remaining doors. Since you initially chose A with 1/5 probability, and there are 3 remaining doors, each gets 1/5 + (1/5)/3 = 1/5 + 1/15 = 4/15 of the probability. But more simply: the 4/5 probability that the prize wasn't behind A gets concentrated in the 3 remaining doors, so each has 4/5 ÷ 3 = 4/15 probability, while A still has 1/5.

### Exercise 32: Sundays and Mondays in a 365-Day Year

**Problem:** Non-leap year with 365 days, January 1 is Monday.

**a) How many Sundays are in the year?**

**Solution:**
A non-leap year has 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 extra day.
Since there are 7 days in a week, there are 52 full weeks = 52 Sundays.
The extra day means one day of the week appears 53 times instead of 52.
Since January 1 is Monday, the extra day is also Monday.
Therefore: Sundays appear 52 times.

**b) How many Mondays are in the year?**

**Solution:**
As above, there are 52 full weeks = 52 Mondays, plus the extra Monday (since Jan 1 is Monday).
Therefore: Mondays appear 53 times.
